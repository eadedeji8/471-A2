Assignment 2: Same Records, Two Stories

CMSC471 White Hat / Black Hat Visualization by Emmanuel Adedeji, Ricky Chen and Jonathan Solomon.

In July 2020 ProPublica published police discipline records that New York had kept secret for decades. The file holds allegations of misconduct from complaints against New York City police officers. Each one was investigated by the city's Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB). Both visualizations below read that one file and ask the same two questions. Do complaints hold up? Are they becoming more or less common?

White hat write-up

Dataset and audience. ProPublica's file lists one allegation per row: the type of alleged misconduct (force, abuse of authority, discourtesy or offensive language), the year the complaint arrived, and how the CCRB ruled. We wrote for a general reader who has never heard the board's vocabulary, and we asked what that reader would ask first: when the board finished an investigation, what did it decide?

Marks and encodings. The ruling is a nominal attribute, so it gets hue, an identity channel. Shares and counts are quantitative, so they get bar length on a common scale, the most precise magnitude channel in Mackinlay's effectiveness ranking. The first chart is a 100% stacked bar because the task is part to whole across types. Substantiated starts at the left edge and exonerated ends at the right edge, so the two rulings a reader compares most each sit on a shared baseline. Orange and blue stay distinct under color vision deficiency, and gray marks unsubstantiated because it is the ruling where the board reached no conclusion. Every segment wide enough prints its share, so the main values do not depend on color or hover. The second chart uses bars from zero because counts are ratio data.

Data transformation. We grouped 11 disposition labels into 3 outcomes, counted allegations by type and by year, and converted counts to shares of each group. The share button normalizes every year to 100% so that years with very different volumes can be compared. We flag weak data instead of hiding it: 2019 and low-count years are faded, not dropped.

Titles and labels. Each title states the finding in a sentence. The legend defines each ruling in plain words, because "exonerated" sounds like "did not happen" and means the opposite. Axis titles name the unit, allegations, since one complaint holds several. Row labels give examples of each type. The two notes on the yearly chart sit exactly over the bars a reader would otherwise misread.

Source and bias. The largest risk in this file is who it leaves out, so the page says it before the first chart. The CCRB's 2019 annual report says 89% of active officers had never had a substantiated complaint. This file covers the other 11%. It also holds only full investigations, while 58% of complaints the board closed in 2019 were cut short before a ruling. So the 25% substantiated share here is about twice the board's own 12% for all allegations it ruled on in 2019, and the climb in yearly counts reflects which officers are still employed, not more misconduct.

Interaction. The type buttons filter the yearly chart, the second pair of buttons reconfigures it between counts and shares, and tooltips give details on demand. These follow the filter, reconfigure and elaborate intents from Yi et al.

Take the black hat off

Each button undoes one trick and keeps the earlier fixes.

This is the graphic as a reader would meet it. Nothing in it is false. Step through the buttons to see what it hides.

"Not substantiated" was two different rulings in one color. Unsubstantiated means the board could not decide. Exonerated means the act happened and NYPD rules allowed it. Neither means the complaint was false. The allegations the board did rule false, called unfounded, were removed before the file was published. The substantiated share was drawn as an empty track, and now has a color of its own.

The 3-year average hid a single bad year. The real counts are close to flat from 2010 to 2018 and then drop once, in 2019. Averaging each year with the two before it spread that one drop over three points, so it looked like a steady decline.

The axis was fitted to the data instead of starting at zero. That made the line cross most of the chart's height. From zero, ten years of complaints look almost level until the last point.

The window opened on the highest year and closed on an unfinished one. The file only holds closed cases. Complaints received in 2019 run from 69 in January down to 6 in December, because later cases were still open. The full series also shows why no trend here can be trusted: early years are low because most officers from then have left the force and are not in the file.

Black hat write-up

Goal. The graphic argues that complaints rarely hold up and are dying out. We limited ourselves to tricks that keep every plotted number true, because Correll and Heer point out that the hardest attacks to catch stay faithful to the data. A reader who checks our arithmetic will find it correct.

Data transformation.

  • Merged categories. "Not substantiated" adds unsubstantiated (no conclusion) to exonerated (it happened, and the rules allowed it). Together they make 75%. Neither ruling says the complaint was false, and the one ruling that does, unfounded, is not in the file at all.
  • Hidden selection. Every officer in the file has at least one substantiated allegation. A file chosen that way cannot show that complaints fail.
  • A different unit in each chart. The left chart counts allegations, where 25% are substantiated. Counting complaints would give 32%. The right chart counts complaints, which fall. Allegations per year rose 30% from 2009 to 2018.
  • Cherry-picked window. The line starts in 2009, the highest year, and ends in 2019, when most late cases were still open and so missing from the file.
  • Smoothing. A 3-year average is a normal way to reduce noise. Here it spreads the unfinished 2019 count across the last three points, which turns one artifact into a downward trend. From 2009 to 2018 the real count fell 11%.

Marks and encodings.

  • Fitted axis. The y-axis runs from 550 to 750. The encoding lecture shows cases where a zero baseline flattens a real change, so an axis that starts above zero passes a first look. Here the line crosses 75% of the chart's height for a 21% change in the value.
  • Color that erases. Both merged rulings share one navy with no seam between them, and the substantiated share is the same pale gray as an empty track, so misconduct findings look like nothing is there.
  • Sort order. Force leads at 87% and sets the reader's anchor.

Titles and labels.

  • A headline the data cannot support. "Complaints are falling" and "complaints against NYPD officers" both claim the whole city. The file holds 449 complaints for 2019. The CCRB received 4,959 that year.
  • Borrowed trust. The source line names the CCRB and ProPublica, the footnote gives a definition of "not substantiated" that is accurate and still hides the merge, and the tooltips give exact numbers. Hullman and Diakopoulos call this provenance rhetoric: signals of care that make a reader stop checking.

Why it is hard to catch. No single choice breaks a rule a reader can point to. The merged label is accurate, the average is labeled, and the window is "the last decade". The deception sits in what the file leaves out, which a reader can only learn from ProPublica's notes, not from the chart.

Extra credit features

  • Undo the black hat, one trick at a time. The five buttons under the black hat graphic remove each trick in turn and keep the earlier fixes. The bars, axes, titles and headline numbers animate between states, and a caption explains each change. A reader sees how much each trick contributed, which a static before-and-after pair cannot show. It goes beyond the requirement of a separate write-up by making the critique part of the visualization.
  • Linked white hat views. Clicking a type in the first chart, or a type button, filters the yearly chart and fades the other rows. A second pair of buttons switches the yearly chart between counts and shares of each year, with animated transitions.
  • Honest handling of weak data. Instead of dropping unreliable years, the white hat fades 2019 and any year with fewer than 100 allegations, and puts a note over each range explaining why.
  • Blind reader testing with AI. We tested the black hat on separate AI sessions that saw only a screenshot, one as an ordinary reader and one as an expert, and used their answers to make it subtler. The AI usage section describes the method and results.

Development process

How the work was split. All three of us explored the data together. Ricky built the white hat visualization. Emmanuel built the black hat visualization. Jonathan wrote the write-ups and recorded the demo video.

Time spent. About 7 people-hours in total, with each of us spending 2 to 3 hours.

What took the most time. Iterating on AI-generated drafts until they met our standard took the most time. Some charts were also hard to build by hand while keeping to the expressiveness and effectiveness principles from lecture: each encoding had to show all the facts in the data and only those, using the most precise channel for each attribute.

AI usage

We used Claude Code, an AI coding tool, in four ways.

Research. It read ProPublica's notes and the CCRB's 2019 annual report and listed what the file leaves out, with a link for each fact. We checked each number against its source before using it in a write-up. This is where the selection problem came from, and that problem ended up shaping both visualizations.

Data checks. It wrote short Python scripts to profile the CSV. Two findings changed our design. Complaints received in 2019 fall from 69 in January to 6 in December, which showed us that 2019 is incomplete. Few records before 2000 survive because most of those officers have left the force. Every number quoted on this page was recomputed from the data file.

Code. It drafted the D3 code. Its first draft used JavaScript modules and helper functions we had not covered in class. We had it rewrite everything to follow our lab files: one init() that loads the CSV, join() with enter, update and exit, and the same tooltip pattern. We also removed page styling we did not like.

Testing the black hat on blind readers. This was the most useful part. We gave a screenshot of the black hat graphic, and nothing else, to separate AI sessions. One played an ordinary newspaper reader and one played a visualization expert looking for tricks. On our first version the reader concluded "most complaints against NYPD cops don't hold up", which was the goal. The expert caught the axis that started at 400 under a shaded area right away, and rated the graphic 3 out of 5 for how obvious the tricks were. We replaced the shading and the hand-picked floor with a 3-year average and an axis fitted to the data, which is what many chart tools do by default. The expert then rated it 2 out of 5. Even the expert could only suspect, not confirm, that the data left out most officers and that 2019 was incomplete, because neither shows in the chart. That matches what Correll and Heer say about attacks that stay faithful to the data.

What we did not hand over. We chose the dataset and the questions, approved each black hat trick before it went in, and reread every section to check its numbers and its wording.
