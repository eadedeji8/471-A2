Assignment 2: Same Records, Two Stories

CMSC471 White Hat / Black Hat Visualization by Emmanuel Adedeji, Ricky Chen and Jonathan Solomon.

In July 2020 ProPublica published police discipline records that New York had kept secret for decades. The file holds allegations of misconduct from complaints against New York City police officers. Each one was investigated by the city's Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB). Both visualizations below read that one file and ask the same two questions. Do complaints hold up? Are they becoming more or less common?

White hat: what the review board decided

Before reading the charts, know who is in this data. It only covers officers who were still on the force in June 2020 and had at least one substantiated allegation. That is officers, about 1 in 9. It only covers complaints the board fully investigated and closed. ProPublica also removed the allegations the board ruled unfounded, about 9% of what it received. So the shares below describe this group of officers. They are not rates for all complaints or all officers.

Take the black hat off

Each button undoes one trick and keeps the earlier fixes.

This is the graphic as a reader would meet it. Nothing in it is false. Step through the buttons to see what it hides.

"Not substantiated" was two different rulings in one color. Unsubstantiated means the board could not decide. Exonerated means the act happened and NYPD rules allowed it. Neither means the complaint was false. The allegations the board did rule false, called unfounded, were removed before the file was published. The substantiated share was drawn as an empty track, and now has a color of its own.

The 3-year average hid a single bad year. The real counts are close to flat from 2010 to 2018 and then drop once, in 2019. Averaging each year with the two before it spread that one drop over three points, so it looked like a steady decline.

The axis was fitted to the data instead of starting at zero. That made the line cross most of the chart's height. From zero, ten years of complaints look almost level until the last point.

The window opened on the highest year and closed on an unfinished one. The file only holds closed cases. Complaints received in 2019 run from 69 in January down to 6 in December, because later cases were still open. The full series also shows why no trend here can be trusted: early years are low because most officers from then have left the force and are not in the file.
